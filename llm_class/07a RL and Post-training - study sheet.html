Kyle's vocabulary:
  • Supervised instruction tuning = SFT
  • Preference data = humans ranking outputs
  • Reward model = a neural net that imitates those rankings
  • Reinforcement learning = trial and error against a score

SEGMENT 1Post-training on conversations

A base model isn't an assistant

  • It's an "internet document simulator on the token level." It continues text the way the internet would.
  • Elsewhere Karpathy calls it a "lossy compression of the internet."

Post-training is cheap

  • Pre-training: about 3 months on thousands of computers, costing millions of dollars.
  • SFT: about 3 hours, because the conversation dataset is much smaller than the internet.

SFT = same algorithm, new data

  • Throw out the internet documents and swap in conversations. Only the dataset changes.
  • The model "very rapidly adjusts." It learns answers ("2+2 is 4"), a personality, and to refuse some requests.
  • "Programming by example": behavior is implicitly programmed by the dataset, not written in code.

Conversations become tokens

  • Each conversation becomes a 1-D sequence of tokens, like a protocol (he compares it to TCP/IP).
  • The format (GPT-4o): <|im_start|> role <|im_sep|> message <|im_end|>. "im" stands for "imaginary monologue," and even Karpathy isn't sure why.
  • Special tokens are new, created in post-training. They mark whose turn it is. His 2-turn example came to 49 tokens.
  • When you chat, the server ends the conversation with <|im_start|>assistant<|im_sep|> and samples the reply.

Where the conversations come from

InstructGPT (OpenAI, 2022)
  • Human contractors (Upwork, Scale AI) wrote both the prompts and the ideal answers.
  • They followed labeling instructions to be helpful, truthful, harmless. These run to hundreds of pages.
  • The dataset was never released. Open copies exist, such as OpenAssistant ("explain monopsony… to a dog").
Today
  • Data is mostly synthetic: LLM-generated, then edited by humans.
  • Example: UltraChat, with millions of conversations ("SFT mixtures").
  • After about 100k examples, the model takes on the persona.
"Dispelling the magic": who are you talking to? A statistical simulation of a human labeler following the company's instructions, often a hired expert.
  • Question was in the data (e.g. Paris landmarks): you get something close to what the labeler wrote.
  • Question wasn't in the data: the answer is emergent, combining pre-training knowledge with the post-training style.

SEGMENT 2From SFT to reinforcement learning

  • SFT is algorithmically identical to pre-training. RL is the third stage, still part of post-training.
  • At labs, separate teams handle each stage and hand the model off.

Why not just train on human solutions?

Emily buys 3 apples and 2 oranges. Each orange is $2. Total is $13. What does each apple cost? → $3

  • A solution has two jobs: (1) reach the right answer, and (2) look nice for a human. Focus on (1).
  • Humans don't know which token sequences are best for the LLM:
    • The model gets limited computation per token, so it can't make big leaps. A step like "(13 − 4) ÷ 3 =" in one token invites mistakes.
    • "Our cognition is different." Steps that are trivial for the model just waste tokens.
    • Humans may inject knowledge the model lacks, or fail to use knowledge it has.
  • So: human examples initialize the model, and the model must discover its own paths by trial and error.

How RL works: guess and check

  1. Take a prompt with a known final answer.
  2. The model generates many solutions (thousands, even millions). They differ because sampling is random.
  3. Check each one: 🟢 correct or 🔴 wrong. (His cartoon: 15 tries, 4 correct.)
  4. Train on the successes, e.g. the best one: correct, and maybe the shortest. Those paths become more likely.
  5. Repeat across tens of thousands of prompts, over and over.
  • The solutions come from the model itself, not from humans. It's practicing.
  • SFT initializes the model "into the vicinity of correct solutions," and RL dials it in.
  • Unlike kids, who go chapter by chapter, LLMs do each stage across all textbooks at once.

SEGMENT 3RLHF: reward models, and why they go wrong

Verifiable domains
  • There's a concrete correct answer: math, code, Go.
  • Checked automatically with a boxed answer or an LLM judge. No humans needed.
Unverifiable domains
  • No single right answer: jokes, poems, creative writing, summaries.
  • Example: "write a joke about pelicans."

The problem: RL needs a huge number of scores. 1,000 updates × 1,000 prompts × 1,000 attempts = 1 billion human ratings, which is impossible.

The fix is RLHF (from an OpenAI paper, many of whose authors later co-founded Anthropic). The core trick is indirection: train a reward model to simulate human preferences, then run RL against that simulator as many times as you like.

Training the reward model

  1. The model writes, say, 5 jokes for one prompt.
  2. A human ranks them from best to worst. Ranking is easier than giving exact scores.
  3. The reward model takes prompt + one joke and outputs one number (0 = worst, 1 = best). It's probably a Transformer, but a scorer, not a text generator.
  4. It is nudged until its scores agree with the human ranking. Humans only rank about 1,000 prompts × 5 = 5,000 jokes.
Upsides
  • Lets RL work in any domain, including unverifiable ones.
  • Models get empirically a bit better.
  • Why, per Karpathy's guess: the discriminator–generator gap. It's much easier to judge than to create, so ranking gives better data.
Downsides
  • The reward model is a lossy simulation of humans.
  • RL games the reward model. Jokes improve for a few hundred updates, then fall off a cliff: "the the the the the" scores 1.0.
  • These are adversarial examples, and there are infinitely many. "You can't win this game."
  • In practice: run a few hundred updates, then crop it and ship.
"RLHF is not RL." It is RL technically, but not the magical kind you can run forever.
  • Verifiable domains can't be gamed, so RL can run indefinitely. Go has a perfect scorer (win or lose), which is how RL beat Lee Sedol.
  • RLHF's reward is gameable, so it's "a little fine-tune" that slightly improves the model. GPT-4o went through RLHF.

Karpathy's wrap-up

  • The three stages are how we teach children, so we're writing textbooks for LLMs. Training uses tens to hundreds of thousands of GPUs.
  • The Swiss cheese model: LLMs are brilliant at many things, with random holes. They can solve Olympiad problems yet stumble on "9.11 vs. 9.9."
  • Use models as tools, for inspiration and first drafts. They hallucinate. You're responsible for the final product.

Small details worth a glance

  • InstructGPT sample prompts: "regain enthusiasm for my career," "top 10 sci-fi books," "translate to Spanish."
  • SFT assistants hallucinate. Two fixes: tools like web search (for recent info) and a code interpreter.
  • RL demo: Gemma 2 (2B) got the apple problem right every time, with different wording, because sampling is random.
  • Good RL solutions "don't make mental leaps" and "fully utilize the knowledge of the model."
  • "Humor is secretly very difficult." The pelican jokes were bad.

Traps

  • ✗ Post-training is the most expensive stage. → Pre-training is.
  • ✗ SFT uses a new algorithm. → Same algorithm, new data.
  • ✗ Personality is programmed in code. → It's programmed by example.
  • ✗ In RL, experts write the solutions. → The model generates its own.
  • ✗ RL = worked examples. → That's SFT. RL = practice problems.
  • ✗ Human solutions are always best for the model. → Its cognition is different.
  • ✗ SFT is useless once you have RL. → It initializes the model.
  • ✗ In RLHF, humans write ideal jokes. → They rank the model's jokes.
  • ✗ Humans score every RL attempt. → The reward model does.
  • ✗ The reward model generates text. → It outputs one score.
  • ✗ Longer RLHF means a better model. → It improves, then collapses.
  • ✗ Patch each adversarial example. → There are infinitely many.
  • ✗ RLHF is best for math. → Math is verifiable, so regular RL works there. RLHF is for unverifiable tasks.
  • ✗ Go is unverifiable. → It has a perfect scorer (win or lose).

Optional readings (gist only, not drilled)

  • OpenAI on sycophancy: an update became too agreeable after thumbs-up/down data was added as a reward. It's real-world reward gaming: people thumbs-up flattery.
  • Lambert, RLHF book ch. 1: the recipe is instruction tuning → preference tuning → RL with verifiable rewards.
  • InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022): the recipe is demonstrations → SFT, then rankings → reward model, then RLHF. The 1.3B InstructGPT was preferred over the 175B GPT-3, despite 100× fewer parameters.

The whole class in 5 lines

  1. PREPre-training (exposition) produces a base model, an internet document simulator. Post-training makes it an assistant and is much cheaper.
  2. SFTSame algorithm, new data: conversations written by labelers (now mostly synthetic). ChatGPT ≈ a simulation of a labeler.
  3. RLPractice problems: generate many attempts, check them, train on the winners. The model finds paths that suit its own cognition. SFT initializes, RL dials in.
  4. RLHFFor unverifiable tasks: humans rank, a reward model imitates them, and RL runs against it. It works because judging is easier than creating.
  5. !But RL games the reward model ("the the the" = 1.0), so you stop after a few hundred steps. "RLHF is not RL." Only verifiable domains (Go) allow unlimited RL. Swiss cheese: check the model's work.
