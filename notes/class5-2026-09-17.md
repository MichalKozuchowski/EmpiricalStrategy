## First Principles of Data Visualization
- Goal: show the data, not hide it; graphs should serve the story, not the other way around
- Outliers make histograms ugly but reveal real data problems; fix the outlier, then replot
- Exploratory graphs (for yourself) look different from communication graphs (for an audience)
- Always state units and scale for any quantitative display
- Proportionality rule: graph real estate devoted to an element should match its quantitative importance
- "Lie factor" concept (from Tufte): excess ink devoted to a small effect makes it look large
- Graphic design, colors, and shapes should serve the data, not aesthetic preferences

## Spotting Bad Graphs
- Solar radiation vs. stock prices: convincing figure, but no plausible causal mechanism
- Policy line graph example (from Reddit's r/DataIsWeird):
  - Line graph used for discrete policy categories, implying false continuity
  - Sample of 1,000 split across 4 categories with no breakdown shown, no standard errors
- Key BS-detector triggers: missing units, no sample breakdown, no error bars, implied causality without basis

## One-Dimensional Graphs: Histograms and Density Plots
- Histogram of 2015 preschool enrollment: massive outlier ($1,484 kids) compresses all other variation into one bar
  - Fix: winsorize at 99.5th percentile using `.clip()` in Python; creates a mass point at the cutoff
- Iterative LLM workflow: paste error or figure into ChatGPT, ask for a drop-in replacement chunk of code
- Grouped histograms by star rating: overlapping distributions, hard to read
  - Switched to ridge density plots with median (dotted) and mean (solid) lines per group
  - Density normalizes across groups for comparability but loses count information
  - Box plots show the same information; better for small datasets where smoothing is misleading
- Histogram vs. density tradeoff:
  - Histogram preserves counts but buries minority groups when one group dominates
  - Density enables cross-group comparison but obscures relative group sizes
- Stacked bar chart (suggested by student): valid alternative, shows fraction of schools by price bucket and star rating; communicates similar story to ridge plot

## Two-Dimensional Graphs: Scatter Plots, Bin Scatter, Line Plots, Bar Charts
- Scatter plot (college ratio vs. median income):
  - Reveals census truncation at $250k median household income, an artifact missed before plotting
  - Convention: x-axis is the cause, y-axis is the effect
  - Add regression line in a contrasting color; display coefficient and standard error on the figure
- Bin scatter: groups x-axis into quantiles, plots mean of y per quantile
  - Cleaner than raw scatter; also reveals non-linearity if present
- Line plots: base everything at 100 in the first year (2010) to compare trends across groups on the same scale
  - Star 4 schools roughly doubled (270 to 616, ~250% increase); star 1 flat
  - Faceting by star rating helps but loses cross-group comparability; indexed lines solve both
- Bar charts: order bars by the variable of interest; side-by-side bars (2010 vs. 2018) show change clearly
- Minard's Napoleon map: cited as one of the best graphs ever made; encodes army size, direction, geography, and temperature in one figure

## Geographic and Multi-Dimensional Maps
- Shapefiles needed for geographic plots: point data (school ID + lat/long) and polygon data (block groups, counties)
  - Shapefiles uploaded to Canvas under `data/preschool/shapefiles`
  - Requires geopandas; install via `pip install geopandas` if missing
- Pittsburgh map example: block groups colored by college-educated household share; preschools overlaid
  - High-quality schools (red diamonds) cluster near the dense, college-educated central area
  - Fewer schools and fewer high-quality schools toward the southeast
- Hexagonal grid alternative: replaces irregular block group polygons with uniform hexagons
  - Reduces distraction from shape irregularity; patterns of variation become clearer
