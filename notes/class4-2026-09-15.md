## Missing Data: Frameworks
Three patterns (same MCAR/MAR/MNAR structure as the slides):
1. **MCAR**: no systematic cause; data stays representative, but missing products/rows can still
   distort demand estimates even when missingness itself is unbiased
2. **MAR**: systematic, but based on something *observable* (e.g. missingness varies by gender in
   a clinical trial — you can see and control for it)
3. **MNAR**: selected on the *unobserved* variable itself (e.g. patients dropping out of a trial
   specifically because the drug isn't working for them)

**You cannot test which pattern applies** — only check whether the pattern you see is *consistent*
with one story or another. It's always an assumption, never a proof.

## Missing Data: Preschool Dataset
- Variables with missing data: prices, enrollment, capacity, number of teachers.
- Diagnosis via groupby on county, year, and star rating:
  - **Geography**: no clear pattern — the variance we saw across counties is mostly driven by
    small sample sizes in individual counties, not a real geographic effect
  - **Year**: a slight upward trend in missingness in earlier years
  - **Star rating**: the **strongest signal by far** — 1-star schools have far higher missingness
- **Mechanism**: Pennsylvania's rating system requires higher-rated schools to submit more data;
  lower-rated schools simply aren't asked to report enrollment.
- **Professor's classification: classic MNAR.** The reasoning: missingness is driven by the very
  quality variable (star rating) that is the object of study itself. Even though rating is
  technically an *observed* column, the paper's whole purpose is studying quality dynamics — so
  treating rating as a neutral, controllable observable understates the problem. This is a
  stricter standard than the textbook MAR/MNAR distinction on paper: when the variable driving
  missingness *is* the outcome of interest, it gets treated with MNAR-level caution rather than
  assumed safe just because you can see it.

## Outliers: Detection and Treatment
- Check via `describe()` with custom percentiles (5th, 25th, 50th, 75th, 99.5th).
- **Preschool price findings**: median ~$34/day (plausible), min $2 (likely an outlier vs. the
  0.5th percentile of ~$15), max $249 (could be a weekly-rate reporting error, or a genuinely
  premium school — ambiguous, worth checking corroborating variables before deciding).
- **Enrollment**: max of ~1,484 children flagged as implausible. Rule of thumb: if enrollment
  exceeds capacity, cap it at capacity.
- Winsorize roughly 0.5th–99.5th percentile rather than dropping.
- **Do not drop outliers automatically** — think about:
  - Whether the analysis depends on the tail (e.g. a luxury-segment question needs the top tail)
  - Whether the same firm consistently reports the same anomalous value (systematic, not a fluke)
  - Whether other variables (firm name, capacity) corroborate or contradict the outlier
- Other outlier sources to watch for generally: mischievous survey respondents, corrupted sensor
  measurements, speed-running/inattentive respondents.

## Supply and Demand: Key Patterns
- STAR rating distribution shifts over time: **1-star share falls from 65% to 53%**; **4-star
  share nearly doubles to ~20%**.
  - **4-star is an absorbing state**: schools rarely exit the market once they reach it.
- Higher-rated schools charge higher prices, have larger capacity, and higher enrollment.
- Block-group demand (low/mid/high enrollment terciles): higher enrollment share correlates with
  higher median income, rent, property values, and share of college-educated parents.
- After merging supply and demand: high-price preschools operate in higher-income, higher-rent,
  more educated neighborhoods. Accredited schools sit in neighborhoods averaging **~$63k median
  income vs. ~$56k for non-accredited**.
- **Takeaway**: product quality and consumer demographics are systematically sorted together —
  this sorting is a critical input for demand estimation (you can't treat "who buys the
  high-quality product" as random).

## Merging Datasets
- Merge `df_preschools` and `df_blockgroup` on **GIS_join and year**.
- Two things to check *before* merging:
  1. **ID mismatches**: neighborhoods with no preschools will drop out of a preschool-anchored
     merge — acceptable or not depending on the question you're asking.
  2. **Duplicates**: duplicate IDs inflate the merged dataset — `assert` uniqueness on
     firm-year and block-group-year *before* merging, not after.
- **Sanity check**: the merged dataset should have the **same row count as the original preschool
  dataset** (confirms the left-merge didn't silently duplicate or drop preschool rows).
